The Hidden Work Behind a Data Science Project: 7 Steps Before You Build Your First Model

A practical look at the decisions, checks and preparation that make a project stronger before the first algorithm is trained.

A data science project rarely begins with choosing Random Forest, tuning a neural network or writing a model-training script. The most important work often happens earlier: understanding the decision, finding suitable data, checking what the data can and cannot tell you, and shaping a workflow that can be trusted.

Skipping this groundwork can lead to a polished notebook that answers the wrong question. A thoughtful pre-model process helps you avoid wasted experiments and makes your final results easier to explain, reproduce and use.

What happens before modeling?

  1. Turn a broad request into a measurable problem.
  2. Identify relevant and permitted data sources.
  3. Audit the dataset's structure and quality.
  4. Explore distributions, relationships and unusual patterns.
  5. Prepare features without leaking future information.
  6. Choose a validation plan that matches the data.
  7. Organize the project so each step is traceable.

1. Translate the Business Request Into a Specific Question

Stakeholders often describe a goal in broad language: reduce customer churn, improve sales, identify risky transactions or forecast demand. Before collecting data, clarify what decision the analysis is expected to support.

For a churn project, for example, “predict customers who may leave” still leaves several unanswered questions:

  • What counts as churn: cancellation, inactivity or non-renewal?
  • How far ahead should the risk be predicted?
  • Who will use the prediction, and what action can they take?
  • How frequently will predictions be generated?
  • What is more costly: missing a likely churner or contacting a customer unnecessarily?

Write a short problem statement that defines the population, outcome, prediction window, intended action and constraints. This becomes the reference point for every later technical decision.

Practical output: A one-paragraph problem brief and a small set of measurable success criteria—not a list of algorithms.

2. Find Data That Can Actually Answer the Question

Once the question is clear, identify where the required information may exist. Sources could include internal databases, application event logs, public datasets, surveys, APIs or files supplied by a business team. The source must be relevant to the question, available at the required level of detail and permitted for the intended use.

Check the data's ownership, collection method, update frequency, time coverage, granularity and known limitations. A customer-level prediction needs records that can be connected to customers consistently. A daily forecast needs a dependable time series with enough historical coverage.

Also record what is unavailable. If the dataset does not contain customer support interactions, for example, the project cannot claim to evaluate how support experience affects churn.

Create a simple source inventory

  • Source name and responsible owner
  • What one row represents
  • Key identifiers and date fields
  • Refresh frequency and historical coverage
  • Access restrictions, privacy requirements and usage permissions
  • Known gaps or collection changes

3. Audit the Dataset Before Trusting It

A file opening successfully does not mean its contents are analysis-ready. Inspect dimensions, column names, data types, missing values, duplicates, category consistency and value ranges. Confirm that identifiers behave as expected and that date fields use consistent formats.

import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("customer_data.csv")

print("Shape:", df.shape)
print("\nColumn types:")
print(df.dtypes)

print("\nMissing values:")
print(df.isna().sum().sort_values(ascending=False).head(10))

print("\nDuplicate rows:", df.duplicated().sum())
print("\nUnique values:")
print(df.nunique().sort_values().head(10))

print("\nNumeric summary:")
print(df.describe().T)

These checks are a starting point, not an automatic cleaning recipe. A missing age value may need a different treatment from a missing transaction timestamp. Duplicate rows may be accidental copies—or legitimate repeated events. Investigate the reason before deleting or replacing anything.

Ask questions behind every data issue

  • Does a missing value mean “unknown,” “not applicable” or a failed collection?
  • Are extreme values genuine events or data-entry errors?
  • Do categories differ only because of spelling, spacing or capitalization?
  • Did the schema or collection process change over time?
  • Are there records that should never be included in the target population?

4. Explore Patterns Before Deciding What to Transform

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) helps you understand the shape and context of the dataset. Start with distributions for important numeric fields, frequency counts for categories and trends over time. Then compare variables that may relate to the target or the business question.

Useful questions include:

  • Are numeric variables heavily skewed or concentrated around a few values?
  • Are some categories extremely rare?
  • Does the target vary by customer segment, region or time period?
  • Are there seasonal patterns, sudden shifts or collection gaps?
  • Do two columns appear to encode nearly the same information?

Use histograms, box plots, bar charts, time-series plots and carefully selected cross-tabs to investigate. A correlation matrix can reveal linear associations, but correlation alone does not establish causation or prove that a variable is useful.

Keep a record of discoveries: Note the observation, supporting chart or statistic, possible explanation and follow-up check. This prevents EDA from becoming a collection of plots without a conclusion.

5. Prepare Features With the Prediction Moment in Mind

Feature preparation is not simply filling null values and converting text into numbers. Every feature must be available at the moment a real prediction would be made.

Imagine predicting whether a customer will cancel within the next 30 days. A column containing the eventual cancellation date cannot be used as an input for a prediction made today. Likewise, a “total complaints” feature is unsafe if it includes complaints submitted after the prediction date.

Define a clear observation window and target window. Create features only from information available inside the observation window. For categorical variables, decide how unseen categories will be handled. For numeric fields, choose transformations based on the distribution and the model's needs—not simply because a technique is popular.

Where possible, put imputation, scaling and encoding inside a preprocessing pipeline. Fit these transformations on training data only, then apply the learned transformations to validation and test data. This reduces the risk of information leaking across the split.

6. Design Validation Before Comparing Algorithms

The validation method should reflect how the project will be used. Randomly splitting every dataset is not always appropriate. If records belong to the same customer, keep related records together when necessary. If the goal is to predict future events, train on earlier periods and validate on later ones. If observations are independent, a stratified split may help preserve class proportions.

Before modeling, write down:

  • What data belongs in training, validation and final testing?
  • What unit must remain grouped, such as customer, patient, store or device?
  • Does the split preserve the time direction of the real task?
  • Which metric reflects the cost of the project's errors?
  • What simple baseline will the model need to beat?

Keep the final test set untouched while selecting features and tuning models. Repeatedly checking it turns the test set into part of the development process and makes the final score less independent.

7. Build a Workflow That Another Person Can Follow

Good project organization makes the analysis easier to review and update. Separate raw data, processed data, notebooks, reusable source code, reports and configuration. Avoid editing the only copy of a raw dataset. Record package versions and random seeds where they affect reproducibility.

A simple structure could look like this:

customer-churn-project/
├── data/
│   ├── raw/
│   └── processed/
├── notebooks/
│   ├── 01_data_audit.ipynb
│   └── 02_exploratory_analysis.ipynb
├── src/
│   ├── data_checks.py
│   └── preprocessing.py
├── reports/
│   └── figures/
├── requirements.txt
└── README.md

Do not commit credentials, private customer records or datasets you are not authorized to redistribute. Explain how to obtain approved data, or provide a safe sample or synthetic dataset when sharing the project publicly.

Pre-Model Readiness Checklist

  • Problem statement identifies the decision, target and prediction horizon.
  • Data sources, owners, permissions and limitations are documented.
  • Rows, columns, data types, missingness and duplicates have been inspected.
  • Important distributions and time patterns have been explored.
  • Features are available at the intended prediction moment.
  • Preprocessing is designed to avoid train-test leakage.
  • Validation strategy reflects time, groups and the real use case.
  • Project folders and reproduction instructions are understandable.

Final Thoughts

The quality of a data science model depends heavily on the work completed before training begins. A well-framed question, trustworthy data, careful exploration, leakage-aware features and a realistic validation plan give your modeling experiments a solid foundation. They also make it easier to explain why the result should be trusted—and where its limits remain.
