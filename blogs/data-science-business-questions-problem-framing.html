How to Turn Business Questions into Data Science Problems: A Practical Step-by-Step Guide

Learn to move from a vague business request to a focused, measurable data science project—with a practical example and Python workflow.

A business team rarely starts with a perfectly defined data science problem. A sales manager might say, “We need to improve revenue.” A product team might ask, “Why are users leaving?” These are important questions, but neither one tells a data scientist exactly what to measure, which data to collect, or what kind of analysis to perform.

The first job is therefore not to choose an algorithm. It is to understand the decision the business needs to make and translate that need into a question that data can help answer. Strong problem framing prevents teams from building technically impressive models that solve the wrong problem.

1. Start with the business decision, not the algorithm

Ask what decision will change after the analysis. “Increase revenue” is an outcome, not yet an analytical task. The team may need to decide which customer groups to target, which products to promote, or where a sales process is losing opportunities.

Use a short discovery conversation to clarify:

  • What is happening today, and why is it a concern?
  • Who owns the decision and who will use the result?
  • Which action could the business take based on the analysis?
  • What is the time period and business area in scope?
  • What constraints exist, such as budget, capacity, privacy or timing?

For example, replace “increase sales” with “identify customer groups with a high likelihood of purchasing again in the next 60 days so the marketing team can prioritize retention campaigns.” This version identifies an audience, an outcome, a time horizon and a possible action.

2. Convert the goal into a measurable question

A useful analytical question is specific enough to investigate and measurable enough to evaluate. It should describe the population, outcome and time window. Avoid questions that combine several unrelated goals.

Consider an online retailer concerned about repeat purchases:

  • Business goal: Improve repeat-purchase revenue.
  • Analytical question: Which active customers are most likely to place another order within 60 days?
  • Potential action: Prioritize relevant retention messages for suitable customer groups.

Notice that the analytical question does not promise that a model will increase revenue. It defines something the team can estimate and then test through a business action.

3. Choose KPIs and success criteria

KPIs connect analysis to business outcomes. Select one primary measure and a small number of supporting measures. Define each metric precisely, including its numerator, denominator, time window and eligible population.

MetricWhat it tells youExample definition
Repeat-purchase rateShare of eligible customers who purchase againCustomers with another order in 60 days ÷ eligible customers
Revenue per customerAverage revenue generated per customerNet revenue ÷ customers in the cohort
Campaign conversion rateShare of contacted customers who purchasePurchasers attributed to campaign ÷ delivered contacts
Precision at top KQuality of the highest-ranked customer listActual repeat purchasers among top K ÷ K

For a prediction project, model metrics such as precision, recall, ROC-AUC or PR-AUC can help compare approaches. But the business should also decide what makes the output useful—for example, how many customers the team can contact and the cost of false positives or missed opportunities.

Practical rule: Define success before training a model. Otherwise, teams may select a metric after seeing results and unintentionally favor a model that does not support the real decision.

4. Check whether the required data exists

Translate the analytical question into a data checklist. For a repeat-purchase use case, useful fields may include customer identifiers, order dates, order values, product categories, refunds, marketing contacts and customer status.

Audit the data before committing to a modeling approach:

  • Coverage: Does the dataset include the required customers and time period?
  • Quality: Are dates, identifiers and monetary values valid?
  • Completeness: Which important fields are missing, and why?
  • Consistency: Are customer IDs and order statuses defined consistently across systems?
  • Timeliness: Will the data be available at the moment a prediction or decision is needed?
  • Permissions: Can the team use the data for this purpose under applicable policies?

Also check whether the outcome can be observed reliably. If “repeat purchase” is not consistently recorded, a sophisticated model will not fix the underlying measurement problem.

5. Select the right analytical approach

Not every business question requires machine learning. Choose the simplest method that can answer the question credibly.

Business questionPossible approach
What happened to sales last quarter?Descriptive analysis and dashboards
Why did conversion change?Diagnostic analysis, cohort comparisons and funnel analysis
Which customers may purchase again?Classification or probability ranking, if historical labels are reliable
How much inventory may be needed?Time-series forecasting
Which customers behave similarly?Clustering or rule-based segmentation
Did a new campaign cause improvement?Experiment design or a suitable causal-inference method

Be careful with causal language. A pattern or correlation does not prove that one factor caused another. If the business needs to know whether an intervention caused a change, consider a randomized experiment or a defensible quasi-experimental design.

6. Turn the question into a practical Python workflow

Suppose the retailer has an order table with customer_id, order_date and order_value. The first step is to inspect and summarize the data—not immediately train a classifier.

import pandas as pd

orders = pd.read_csv("orders.csv")

# Parse dates and remove rows without essential identifiers
orders["order_date"] = pd.to_datetime(
    orders["order_date"], errors="coerce"
)
orders = orders.dropna(
    subset=["customer_id", "order_date", "order_value"]
)

# Keep valid, non-negative order values
orders = orders[orders["order_value"] >= 0]

# Basic coverage and quality checks
print("Rows:", len(orders))
print("Customers:", orders["customer_id"].nunique())
print("Date range:", orders["order_date"].min(),
      "to", orders["order_date"].max())

# Summarize customer activity
customer_summary = (
    orders.groupby("customer_id")
    .agg(
        order_count=("order_date", "count"),
        last_order=("order_date", "max"),
        total_spend=("order_value", "sum")
    )
    .reset_index()
)

print(customer_summary.head())

This creates a basic customer-level view. It is not yet a valid prediction dataset: a predictive project must define an observation date, construct features using only information available at that date, and create a future outcome label using a separate prediction window.

For example, choose a historical cutoff date. Calculate customer features from the 90 days before that cutoff, then label whether each customer made another purchase in the following 60 days. Repeat this process across suitable historical cutoffs when enough data is available. Keep training and test periods chronological to avoid using future information.

7. Write a one-page problem statement

Before development begins, document the agreement between business and technical stakeholders. A concise problem statement can use this template:

  • Business context: What challenge or opportunity prompted the work?
  • Decision: What action will the result support?
  • Analytical question: What exactly will be measured, explained or predicted?
  • Population and timeframe: Which customers, products, locations or dates are in scope?
  • Primary KPI: How will business success be assessed?
  • Data sources: Which tables and fields are required?
  • Method: What is the simplest suitable analytical approach?
  • Constraints and risks: What limitations, privacy requirements or assumptions matter?
  • Deliverable: What will stakeholders receive—a report, dashboard, ranked list, forecast or model?

8. Avoid common problem-framing mistakes

  1. Starting with a favorite algorithm: Choose a method only after clarifying the decision and data.
  2. Using vague goals: Replace “improve performance” with a defined outcome and timeframe.
  3. Tracking too many KPIs: Agree on a primary measure and supporting diagnostics.
  4. Ignoring data availability: Validate source systems and label quality before estimating effort.
  5. Confusing prediction with causation: A model that predicts behavior does not automatically explain what will change it.
  6. Skipping a baseline: Compare complex methods with simple rules or existing business processes.
  7. Forgetting implementation: Confirm who will use the result, how often it will be refreshed and what action follows.

9. A final problem-framing checklist

  • Can the business goal be explained in one or two sentences?
  • Is there a clear decision owner and intended action?
  • Is the analytical question measurable and time-bound?
  • Are the primary KPI and success criteria defined?
  • Have data availability, quality and permissions been checked?
  • Does the chosen method match the question?
  • Is there a baseline and a plan for validation?
  • Can stakeholders understand and act on the deliverable?

Conclusion

Good data science begins with a well-framed business problem. By clarifying the decision, defining measurable outcomes, checking data readiness and selecting an appropriate analytical method, you give a project a clear purpose before writing model code. This approach also makes it easier to explain the work, evaluate its value and build solutions that stakeholders can actually use.
